## Key question 1 — Time per job and realistic jobs per day/month (year 1 vs year 2)

### Takeaway
Practitioner-adjacent sources converge on 1.5–3 hours of on-site work for a sofa (corner sofas 2–3 h, a 3-seat 200 cm sofa ~3 h; one detailed claim: 4.5 h including drying), with full drying 1–3 h (up to 24 h to fully dry). Franchise/aggregator sources put a solo mobile cleaner at 3–4 orders per day at full load, but the one first-person account found says the first months bring only 10–20 orders per month and a stable income arrives only after ~1 year (other sources: 6–8 months).

### Inferences
- A workable Yerevan planning assumption: 3-seat sofa ≈ 2 h on site, corner sofa ≈ 2.5–3 h, armchair ≈ 30–45 min (no direct source; scaled from the sofa figures and the US 30–60 min per piece), mattress ≈ 45–60 min; add 30–45 min per call for travel, unloading, water fill/drain and set-up. That gives a physical ceiling of 3 sofa-sized calls (or 2 sofa calls + 2 armchair/mattress add-ons) in a 9–10 h day, matching the "3–4 orders a day" franchise claim only if calls are geographically clustered.
- Demand, not capacity, is the binding constraint in year 1: the only first-person trajectory found (10–20 orders/month in the early months, stability after ~1 year) implies month-1 utilisation of ~15–25% of capacity. A year-2 assumption of 40–60 orders/month (≈2/day on 22–26 working days) is consistent with the "50 orders/month" template and the "2–3 jobs/day at 30 h/week" mature US business.
- Because drying is 1–3 h and customers want the room back, jobs are naturally spaced through the day; a morning + afternoon + evening slot pattern is what the 3-per-day ceiling assumes.

### Gaps
- No practitioner gave separate armchair or mattress minutes; the 30–45 min armchair figure is an inference.
- No source quantified travel/set-up minutes per call in Russia/CIS; the vc.ru car-detailer only gave the fuel cost per call.
- No source gave a month-by-month year-1 vs year-2 order count beyond the T—Ж master's "10–20/month at first, stable after a year".

## Key question 2 — Per-job costs (chemistry, water, electricity, fuel, consumables, depreciation, repair)

### Takeaway
The only Russian per-order cost breakdowns found put chemistry at ≈350 ₽ (≈$4.4) per sofa order and total variable cost at ≈1,000–1,050 ₽ (≈$12.5–13) per call including ≈300 ₽ (≈$3.75) fuel and ≈700 ₽ (≈$8.75) consumables/chemistry — i.e. roughly 10–15% of a 2,500–3,700 ₽ Russian average check (car interiors: 1,000 ₽ cost on an 8,000 ₽ check). Water, electricity, depreciation and repair costs were not quantified by any source found.

### Inferences
- For a Yerevan model, chemistry per 3-seat sofa ≈ $4–6 (Russian practitioner 350 ₽ scaled; imported chemistry in Armenia is unlikely to be cheaper than in Russia), per armchair ≈ $1.5–2 (roughly a third of a sofa's fabric area), per mattress ≈ $3–4. Fuel/taxi per call ≈ $3–5 in-city if the cleaner has a car (300 ₽ Russian benchmark), materially more if relying on taxis with equipment (not sourced — see gaps).
- Water and electricity are negligible per job (a spray-extractor uses a few litres of solution and ≤1.5 kW for ~1–2 h, i.e. cents per job); no source contradicts this, but no source quantifies it either.
- Depreciation: on the low-cost template (≈$700–1,000 of equipment) and 50 jobs/month, straight-line over 3 years ≈ $0.5–0.7 per job; on the $2,000–4,000 franchise-grade kit ≈ $1–2.5 per job. Repair reserve is unsourced; a 5% of revenue reserve is a conservative modelling choice, not a finding.
- Total variable cost per sofa call in Yerevan is therefore plausibly $8–14 (chemistry + fuel + consumables + depreciation) before advertising and tax; per armchair add-on (same call) $2–4.

### Gaps
- No practitioner quantified water, electricity, microfibre/filter replacement, or pump/extractor repair costs; searches for extractor repair costs returned nothing usable.
- The 350 ₽ chemistry figure is from a cleaner who also sells a course; no independent practitioner confirmation was found.
- No figure for the cost of moving equipment by taxi/delivery in the absence of a car.

## Key question 3 — Earnings benchmarks (RU regional cities, Tbilisi, Almaty, Minsk), average check, repeat share, year-1 trajectory, seasonality

### Takeaway
Russian first-person and semi-practitioner sources cluster around 50–90k ₽ (≈$625–1,125) net per month for a solo mobile cleaner once established, 100–130k ₽ (≈$1,250–1,625) gross with two masters, and up to 220k ₽ (≈$2,750) in peak months with a second master; average check 2,500–3,700 ₽ (≈$31–46). Seasonality is sharp: January–February (and July) dead, April–Easter and pre-May-holiday peak with 10-day waiting lists, December busy. ### Inferences
- Established solo net ≈ 25–35 average checks per month in Russia (e.g. 90k ₽ ÷ 3,000 ₽ ≈ 30 net-equivalent orders after costs). Scaled to Yerevan, the writer should compute: net ≈ (Yerevan average check − ~$10–14 variable cost − advertising per order − turnover tax) × orders, using year-1 orders of 10–20/month rising to 40–60/month by year 2.
- Seasonality for a cash-flow model: treat Jan–Feb as ~40–50% of an average month, Mar–Apr and Dec as 130–150%, May and July as 70–80%. (Ratios are inferences from the qualitative practitioner descriptions; Armenian Easter timing and the local pre-New-Year cleaning habit make the same pattern plausible for Yerevan.)
- Franchisee churn "due to few orders" in Belarus and the T—Ж master's slow first year both point to the same warning: the first 6–12 months are marketing-limited, and cash reserves for that period matter more than equipment grade.

## Key question 5 — Armenia: registering an ИП / անհատ ձեռնարկատեր, 2025–2026 tax regime, contributions, cash register, licence

### Takeaway
Registration is same-day at the State Register with a state duty of AMD 3,000–3,500 (≈$8–9), open to foreigners without a residence permit provided they obtain an Armenian social security number. Since 1 Jan 2025 the turnover tax for services is 10% (min 4.5%) with a deduction of 6% of documented expenses, threshold AMD 115M (some sources say 120M); the micro-entrepreneur regime (≤ AMD 24M, no turnover/profit tax/VAT) still exists but excludes Yerevan retail and a widening list of professional services — upholstery/cleaning services do not appear on the exclusion lists quoted, but this must be verified. IEs on turnover tax pay a flat AMD 5,000/month social payment (or 5% of income under the general rule), and consumer-facing service providers must issue fiscal receipts from an SRC-registered cash register (fines AMD 300k first offence).

## Key question 6 — Risks and pitfalls and how practitioners mitigate them

### Takeaway
The dominant financial risk is furniture damage: one wrong water temperature or chemistry concentration "burns" fabric or leaves rings, and "the damage can be several times the profit of the order"; consumer-protection law in the CIS lets customers demand independent expert examination, and contract clauses that bar claims after signing an acceptance act are void. Other documented pitfalls: unpredictable referral flow, franchisees quitting from lack of orders, negative reviews for surprise price rises on arrival, respiratory/musculoskeletal health effects of cleaning chemistry, and price anchoring by competitors advertising "от 990 ₽ / от 140 ₽".

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- For the Yerevan model, treat armchairs, chairs and mattresses as same-call add-ons that raise revenue per stop at near-zero incremental travel cost (the single most important lever per the US "revenue per stop" framing and the gde.expert bundling logic); a plausible planning assumption is that 30–40% of sofa calls add at least one armchair or mattress, but this is not sourced.
- Car interiors are the best cash-flow filler for the winter trough (their demand is less tied to spring cleaning) and use the same extractor, but each job blocks most of a day.
